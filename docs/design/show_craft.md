# Show craft: what eight dramas teach a data screen

Purpose: learn how strong television writers open, explain, withhold and end, then use only the craft. This file copies no dialogue, scene, character, plot or catchphrase into the product. The product names no show. Quotes are five words or fewer. Each claim cites a numbered source at the end. Where I found no reliable source for a point, I say so and do not fill the gap.

## Breaking Bad

- **Open and end.** Each episode is a teaser followed by four acts, a form Vince Gilligan learned on The X-Files. He calls the teaser the subject of "exhaustive discussion", and the room sometimes leaves it blank until a better one comes [1]. Season 2 opens four episodes on black-and-white flash-forwards with one object in colour, and the episode titles, read in order, spell out the finale's event [2][3].
- **Exposition.** Through images and consequence, not speeches. One critic argues the season 2 payoff works through imagery, not plot logic [4].
- **A number, a document, a plan.** The plan is hidden in plain sight: four titles that form a sentence only when lined up [3]. The viewer is given the parts and assembles them.
- **Left unsaid.** What the coloured object means is withheld for a whole season [2]. Gilligan separates "mystery", almost always good, from "confusion", always bad, and once changed an ending to make it less clear [1].
- **Done badly.** The payoff leans on coincidence: one columnist says the show "lost me" in season 2 [5]. Gilligan admits the story gets lost in the weeds and that the team makes continuity mistakes [1].

## Mad Men

- **Open and end.** Episodes close on a period song chosen by Matthew Weiner, a closing note one critic calls a "thematic barometer" [6]. He allows some licence in end-title music but not in music heard inside a scene, and he replaced one song that was out of period [7].
- **Exposition.** Weiner described network pressure to add exposition to a show built on subtext [8]. Characters talk around the thing that matters.
- **A number, a document, a plan.** The first-season finale's pitch is the show's data screen: a slide projector, a set of family photographs, and a sales argument [9]. One analysis argues the direction undercuts the pitch: tight, controlled close-ups of the speaker against loose, messy snapshots on the wall. The words sell a feeling; the pictures show what it costs [9].
- **Left unsaid.** The speaker never names his private loss. The images carry it, so the viewer feels it without being told [9].
- **Done badly.** Daniel Mendelsohn called the writing "extremely weak", the show melodramatic rather than dramatic, and its scenes caption-like. He conceded the pitch episode's emotional power [10]. When a show trusts the image, a speech that explains the image feels like an advert.

## The Sopranos

- **Open and end.** I found no reliable source for a fixed episode-opening formula, so I make no claim about one. The ending is better documented: the finale recasts a familiar family dinner through music and editing, then cuts to black [11]. David Chase said he had "no interest in explaining" it [12], and told one critic "It's all there" [11].
- **Exposition.** The show subverts what television usually sets up; one example is witnesses who die before they can help the authorities [11].
- **A number, a document, a plan.** In one episode a character is lost in the woods and never found. The writer Terence Winter pushed for a later scene to close it; Chase dropped it [11]. Winter's reason for the original choice: not everything gets answered [13].
- **Left unsaid.** The open thread is deliberate and makes the viewer carry the question past the credits [11][13].
- **Done badly.** One critic calls the dream sequences the series' low points [14]. Chase's later remarks about the ending were read as undoing its ambiguity [15]. An author who explains the gap afterwards spends the trust the gap earned.

## The Wire

- **Open and end.** Each episode opens with an on-screen line spoken by a character later in that episode, a hint at the theme [16]. One critic contrasts a season's first and last incidents to show how far its children have fallen [17].
- **Exposition.** David Simon told Nick Hornby that writing for an imagined average reader who needs everything explained makes exposition a burden on the story. Viewers should be trusted to acquire information [18]. Slang is not translated for outsiders [18].
- **A number, a document, a plan.** The statistic is the antagonist. In season 4 a reformer tries to change a police department's culture of manipulating crime numbers, and a homicide unit ignores murders that are not in plain view [17]. The show's lesson is that a number reports what an institution chose to count.
- **Left unsaid.** Who is right is rarely announced; institutions grind down people of integrity, and the viewer draws the conclusion [17].
- **Done badly.** Its demands turn some viewers away; one critic says its long arcs put off the average viewer [19].

## Six Feet Under

- **Open and end.** Every episode opens with a death that usually sets the episode's theme [20][21]. The scene fades to white and a card gives a name and two years, like a headstone [22]. The finale turns the device on the main cast in a flash-forward montage cut to a song [23].
- **Exposition.** Characters talk with the dead. Alan Ball described these as inner dialogue made external [21].
- **A number, a document, a plan.** The card is a whole life reduced to two numbers [22]. The episode then spends an hour showing what the numbers leave out.
- **Left unsaid.** Many of the opening dead are never mentioned again [20]. The death is not explained; its weight is.
- **Done badly.** Season 4's consensus review calls its twists more contrived than compelling [21]. One reviewer says one implausible plot was "piled on top" of another [24].

## Dexter

- **Open and end.** The title sequence shows a morning routine in extreme close-up, so shaving and breakfast look violent. Its director wanted the mundane to look sinister: "mundane yet spectacular" [25].
- **Exposition.** Through constant voice-over narration. Critics later said it spelled out subtext that should have stayed implicit [26].
- **A number, a document, a plan.** The character's code is a rule set that decides who may be harmed and who may not [27]. The rule is stated aloud, often.
- **Left unsaid.** The final season cut back the narration. One critic says that change "failed to land" in the finale [28].
- **Done badly.** The 2013 finale was widely panned [28]. Narration that explains every beat leaves nothing for the viewer to find.

## Boardwalk Empire

- **Open and end.** The pilot opens on an ambush, then jumps back three days to a public speech by the lead [29]. It ends on a montage of killings, cut against the lead bringing flowers to a widow [29]. The title sequence shows a man on a beach as bottles wash ashore and his shoes stay dry. Its designer wanted to "plant some questions" and avoid nostalgia [30].
- **Exposition.** The speech is performance: the audience sees the gap between what he tells the crowd and what he does [31].
- **A number, a document, a plan.** Terence Winter spent months researching the period before he could write [32]. The production decided early to aim for accuracy [33]. The period is a record, not a costume.
- **Left unsaid.** The hypocrisy is never named on screen; the cut does the work [29][31].
- **Done badly.** One review found the pilot "too big", with too many subplots for one episode [29]. Final-season flashbacks were judged redundant by one critic [34].

## Chernobyl

- **Open and end.** The series opens not on the explosion but on a quiet scene two years later: a scientist recording tapes, asking about the cost of lies [35]. The finale returns to that idea of a debt to the truth [36].
- **Exposition.** In the trial a scientist explains the reactor with red cards, which push power up, and blue cards, which push it down [37]. Physics World said viewers came away with a good idea of how a reactor works [37]. Craig Mazin built this from talks with working nuclear scientists [38].
- **A number, a document, a plan.** The most famous number is a lie of the instrument. The reading heard early on was the dosimeter's maximum, not the true dose [39]. The number was real; what it measured was the ceiling.
- **Left unsaid.** The disaster is withheld at the start; the viewer meets the consequence before the cause [35].
- **Done badly.** One scientist character is a composite. Critics say real officials were drawn as villains, and some events were moved in time [40][41]. Mazin said the trial is where he chose dramatic licence over history [42]. A drama about real people inherits a duty that fiction does not.

## Synthesis: five rules that recur

1. **Open on the consequence, then earn the cause.** Teasers, opening deaths, a quiet tape before the explosion, an ambush before the speech [1][20][29][35].
2. **Trust the reader. Cut explanation that the record already shows.** [8][11][18]
3. **A number is only as good as what it measured.** Say what it counted and where it stops: a dosimeter ceiling, a juked statistic, a life as two years [17][22][39].
4. **Mystery, not confusion.** Leave open only what is truly unknown, and make clear that it is open [1][13].
5. **End on the question that moves the reader forward.** An end song, a cut to black, a line that comes back later [6][11][16].

The failures teach a sixth limit: narration that explains everything, coincidence that serves the plot, and real people turned into characters all cost trust [24][26][40].
